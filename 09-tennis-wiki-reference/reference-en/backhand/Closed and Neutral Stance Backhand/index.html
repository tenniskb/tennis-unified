Closed and Neutral Stance Backhand — Technical Reference¶

Your closed stance is the most powerful leg position available to you on the backhand — it produces the greatest weight transfer, the deepest knee bend, and the most complete shoulder coil of any backhand stance you'll use. Your neutral stance is less powerful, but it's what you'll need on faster balls hit toward the middle of the court, where there simply isn't time to set up the full closed stance.

Why the Closed Stance Dominates¶

Four mechanical advantages compound together here. First, a larger step means increased force — your front leg takes a big diagonal step across your body at roughly 45 degrees, and that wider step gives force a longer runway to build over. Second, that wider base allows a deeper knee bend, and a deeper bend stores more elastic energy in your quads and glutes — a bigger stretch-shortening cycle load waiting to be released on your forward drive. Third, you get a genuinely better shoulder turn: your front foot crossing your body forces your hips into a closed position, and that loads your shoulder coil against that hip restriction, maximizing your X-factor separation. Fourth, your hip alignment sets up an inside-out racket path that drives the ball forward with real power and good directional control.

Your Weight Transfer, Step by Step¶

At the end of your backswing, weight loads over your left foot (for a right-hander). As you step forward with your right foot and swing, weight begins transferring. Your right foot needs to hit the ground a split-second before contact — this timing is genuinely critical: grounding early secures your forward momentum, pushes up from the ground, and stabilizes your body enough to help control your arm through impact. After contact, your left foot pivots in a controlled way to the left during your forward swing, rotating your hips slightly and continuing to move energy upward toward your racket.

That pivot needs real calibration. Too much, and your hips open too soon — your arm bends too much at contact and you lose leverage. None at all, and your hips lock, momentum stops, and the shot loses depth. Correct is a smooth, controlled rotation that carries energy upward without rushing your hips open.

Adjusting Direction With Your Step¶

Where your right foot points changes based on where you're sending the ball. Cross-court, your step points more toward the net — roughly 10 to 11 o'clock for a right-hander — pushing your momentum toward that cross-court target. Down the line, your step points closer to 9 to 10 o'clock, more toward the sideline, pushing momentum in that direction instead. These foot-direction choices aren't an afterthought — they need to be set in your preparation, before your swing even starts.

Recovery Timing¶

How fast your recovery step comes depends on how fast you moved into the shot in the first place. Fast lateral movement into your backhand means your recovery happens naturally, early in your follow-through — your own forward momentum carries you into it automatically. A slower or more stationary backhand needs a later, more deliberate recovery step. If you're a recreational player moving and swinging at a more moderate pace, prioritizing stability during the swing itself, with a slightly later, deliberate recovery, generally gives you better racket control at contact.

The Neutral Stance¶

Your neutral stance — right foot stepping more directly toward the net rather than diagonally across your body — is what you need on faster balls hit to the middle of the court, where there simply isn't time to set up the diagonal step. It produces less power than the closed stance, but it's the necessary choice when time's the constraint. You'll notice the difference in your feet: on a neutral-stance backhand, your front foot is less turned and your back foot sits less far behind your body than it would on a closed stance.

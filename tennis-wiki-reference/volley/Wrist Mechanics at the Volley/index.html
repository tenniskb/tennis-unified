Wrist Mechanics at the Volley — Technical Reference¶

Three specific wrist positions have to work together at the exact moment of contact — and together, they build a skeletal buttress behind the ball that no amount of muscular effort can replicate. This is the real anatomical foundation underneath everything else you do at net.

Position One: Ulnar Deviation — Your Skeletal Buttress¶

At contact, your wrist needs to sit in ulnar deviation — tilted toward your pinky side. That specific position lines up the two bones of your forearm, the radius and ulna, directly behind the impact zone. The consequence matters: impact force gets resisted by the actual skeletal structure of your forearm bones, not by muscle. A muscular response to impact — gripping harder, flexing your wrist in reaction — is both slower and weaker than the structural resistance that correct ulnar deviation gives you for free. This is exactly why arming can never substitute for correct wrist position: no amount of muscular bracing produces the skeletal integrity that ulnar deviation creates passively, without you having to think about it.

Position Two: Wrist Extension — The Laid-Back Position¶

On your forehand volley, your wrist stays laid back — in extension, bent slightly backward. This does two things. It exposes your strings to the ball earlier in the contact zone, giving you more time to read and adjust your racket face angle. And it gives you the mechanical leverage you need to actually handle pace — to absorb and redirect a hard-hit ball without your wrist collapsing under it.

Here's the failure mode worth knowing: if your wrist breaks forward at contact instead — flexion instead of extension — your racket head drops. What you get is a floated, soggy return that goes up with no pace and lands short. This is one of the most common volley faults there is, and it's often misdiagnosed as a swing-path problem when it's actually just a wrist position failure.

Position Three: The 90-to-110-Degree Constant¶

The angle between your forearm and your racket face needs to stay somewhere between 90 and 110 degrees throughout the entire contact interval. That constant is what keeps your racket face square to your target regardless of the ball's incoming trajectory. Any flipping or snapping of your wrist during contact — rotation, or a flexion/extension change mid-contact — introduces variables you genuinely can't time reliably at the speeds involved. That 90-to-110 constant needs to be held rigid, through your grip pulse, during the contact window itself. All your adjustments happen in setup, before contact — never during it.

How This Fits With the Still-Wall¶

These three wrist positions are the actual mechanical architecture underneath the still-wall volley's moment of contact. The still-wall model gives you the unit-turn structure that brings your racket to the ball. Your wrist positions ensure the racket face is correctly oriented once it gets there. Neither one works without the other: correct wrist mechanics paired with arming — arm-only movement — still gives you an inconsistent contact point that undermines your wrist positioning's reliability. And a correct still-wall paired with collapsed wrist mechanics means your racket arrives in the right place, but the face angle at impact is wrong anyway.

How to Build This In, In Order¶

Start static — establish ulnar deviation as a grip check before you ever move. Then confirm your wrist extension, your laid-back position, with a mirror or a slow feed. Then drill your grip pulse specifically to ingrain that 90-to-110 constant at contact. Only after your wrist architecture is reliable against a stationary feed should you add movement — your unit turn — back into the mix.
